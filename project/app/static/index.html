⟨ψ⟩ QuantumITS Predicting highway traffic with a quantum computer

Will the highway be busy this hour?
We trained a quantum computer program to answer, and checked it against what really happened.

Cities run Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS): computers that read road sensors and then change traffic lights, warn drivers and suggest detours. To act before a jam forms, the system has to predict traffic. This project tests whether a small quantum program can make that prediction as well as ordinary (classical) machine learning, fast enough to use (under one second).

The problem in four steps

1

The road

A sensor on the I-94 highway between Minneapolis and St Paul (USA) counted the cars passing each hour, together with the weather at that time. That gives 40,564 hours of real data (Oct 2012 – Sep 2018).

Public dataset: “Metro Interstate Traffic Volume”, UCI Machine Learning Repository (also on Kaggle).

2

The question

For a given hour, will more than 3,340 cars pass the sensor? If yes, we call the hour Busy, otherwise Not busy.

3,340 is the middle value of the training years, so about half of all hours are busy.

3

The clues

  • 🕒 Hour of the day
  • 📅 Weekday, or weekend/holiday
  • 🌡️ Temperature
  • 🌧️ Weather (clear → snow)

The model only sees these four clues. It never sees the car count.

4

The fair test

The models learn from 2012–2016. They are then tested on 15,246 hours from 2017–2018 that they have never seen, and each answer is compared with the real car count.

What the data looks like

Average cars per hour over all six years. On weekdays, the morning and evening rush hours are far above the busy line; nights are far below. Weekends are busy only around midday. The model has to learn this pattern, and the effect of weather and temperature, from the clues alone.

Technical notes: what the symbols mean
x = [x₁,x₂,x₃,x₄]One hour’s clues: hour of day, weekend/holiday flag, temperature, weather severity, each scaled to an angle in radians
|ψ(x)⟩ = Uenc(x)|0000⟩Encoding: each clue is written into its own qubit by an RY rotation. Hour uses 2πh/24, so 23:00 sits next to 00:00
Uφ(x) = exp(−iφ(x)H)Feature map (only in the comparison models) with H = ZkZl and φ = (π−xk)(π−xl). It couples pairs of clues, e.g. “rush hour and snow”
U(θ)Trainable rotations RZ·RY·RZ plus a ring of CNOTs. Encode → U(θ) is repeated 3 times (data re-uploading), which lets the model learn curved decision boundaries
⟨Z₀⟩ → P(busy)The measured expectation of qubit 0 (between −1 and +1) is rescaled classically: P = σ(a⟨Z₀⟩ + b). P > 0.5 means “Busy”

Score on all 15,246 test hours

The quantum model is as accurate as the best classical models, not better. That matches the research paper: on this problem, quantum gives a comparable model with very few settings (50), not a speed or accuracy advantage.

Where the quantum model is right and wrong

9 out of 10 mistakes are on hours within 1,000 cars of the busy line, most often around 7 pm, when traffic is winding down and can land on either side of the line.

Look inside the quantum circuit

What the 4 qubits read out

Chance of each of the 16 possible readings |q₀q₁q₂q₃⟩. Only qubit 0 is used for the answer: purple bars have q₀ = 0 and red bars have q₀ = 1. ⟨Z₀⟩ = P(q₀=0) − P(q₀=1) is then turned into P(busy).

The circuit (one of its 3 repeated layers)

RY(w·x) gates load the clues (encoding); the RZ·RY·RZ gates hold the learned settings θ; the CNOTs link the qubits in a ring (entanglement) so the clues can interact.

When the highway is busy, the city can send some groups of cars (“streams”) on a detour. Send too few and the highway jams; send too many and the detour jams. A stream carrying an ambulance 🚑 must stay on the cleared highway. With 8 streams there are 2⁸ = 256 possible plans. A quantum algorithm (QAOA) searches for the plan with the least total delay. Because 256 is small, an ordinary computer can also check every plan, which gives the correct answer to compare the quantum plan with.

Technical notes: how the problem becomes a QUBO

J(x) = (D − d·x)² / Cmain + (d·x)² / Calt + τ·d·x + P·xe

xi = 1 sends stream i on the detour; di is its number of cars (taken from real I-94 counts at that hour), D the total. The first two terms are the delay on each road, which grows with the square of its load; τ is the extra time of the detour; P is a penalty that keeps the ambulance stream xe on the highway. J is quadratic in the 0/1 variables x, so it is a QUBO. Substituting xi = (1−Zi)/2 gives the cost Hamiltonian HC that QAOA minimises. A classical check validates every plan before it is released.

Real quantum chips make mistakes: a qubit can flip on its own (bit-flip, X), get its phase flipped (Z), or both (Y). Error correction spreads one qubit’s information over several qubits so mistakes can be found and undone. Here we store a known value, add random mistakes at the rate you choose, run the correction, and check whether we read back what we stored (the ground truth). The chart shows how often the read-back is wrong.

What each code costs and protects

Each mistake is X, Y or Z with equal chance (depolarising noise, simulated with Qiskit Aer, 6,000 runs × 3 test values). The 3-qubit codes fix only one kind of mistake, so with mixed mistakes they can be worse than no code at all. The 9-qubit Shor code fixes all three kinds.

Which parts of the quantum design matter (500 training hours)

Each row changes one thing in our quantum model. Repeating the encoding (≥ 2 layers) is what makes it work; a single layer gets only 56%.

Effect of hardware noise and of repeated measurements (shots)

Simulated chip noise lowers accuracy only slowly (still ~92% at 5% two-qubit error). A few hundred shots are enough.

Time per prediction vs the 1-second limit

Every stage, including the quantum model, answers in milliseconds, far inside the 1-second budget.
