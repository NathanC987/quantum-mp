⟨ψ⟩ QuantumITS Predicting highway traffic with a quantum computer

Will the highway be busy this hour?
We trained a quantum computer program to answer, and checked it against what really happened.

Cities run Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS): computers that read road sensors and then change traffic lights, warn drivers and suggest detours. To act before a jam forms, the system has to predict traffic. This project tests whether small quantum programs can (1) predict whether the highway will be busy and (2) use that prediction to decide which on-ramps to send on a detour, as well as ordinary (classical) computing and fast enough to use (under one second). Every answer is checked against what really happened.

The problem in five steps

1

The road

A sensor on the I-94 highway between Minneapolis and St Paul (USA) counted the cars passing each hour, together with the weather at that time. That gives 40,564 hours of real data (Oct 2012 – Sep 2018).

Public dataset: “Metro Interstate Traffic Volume”, UCI Machine Learning Repository (also on Kaggle).

2

The question

For a given hour, will more than 3,340 cars pass the sensor? If yes, we call the hour Busy, otherwise Not busy.

3,340 is the middle value of the training years, so about half of all hours are busy.

3

The clues

  • 🕒 Hour of the day
  • 📅 Weekday, or weekend/holiday
  • 🌡️ Temperature
  • 🌧️ Weather (clear → snow)

The model only sees these four clues. It never sees the car count.

4

The fair test

The models learn from 2012–2016. They are then tested on 15,246 hours from 2017–2018 that they have never seen, and each answer is compared with the real car count.

5

From prediction to detours

Cars join the highway at 8 on-ramps. If the hour is predicted busy, the city can tell drivers at some ramps to take a detour road instead. The chance of “busy” from step 2 decides how many ramps to divert.

Each plan is scored by the delay it would really have caused in that hour.

What the data looks like

Average cars per hour over all six years. On weekdays, the morning and evening rush hours are far above the busy line; nights are far below. Weekends are busy only around midday. The model has to learn this pattern, and the effect of weather and temperature, from the clues alone.

x = [x₁,x₂,x₃,x₄]One hour’s clues: hour of day, weekend/holiday flag, temperature, weather severity, each scaled to an angle in radians
|ψ(x)⟩ = Uenc(x)|0000⟩Encoding: each clue is written into its own qubit by an RY rotation. Hour uses 2πh/24, so 23:00 sits next to 00:00
Uφ(x) = exp(−iφ(x)H)Feature map (only in the comparison models) with H = ZkZl and φ = (π−xk)(π−xl). It couples pairs of clues, e.g. “rush hour and snow”
U(θ)Trainable rotations RZ·RY·RZ plus a ring of CNOTs. Encode → U(θ) is repeated 3 times (data re-uploading), which lets the model learn curved decision boundaries

The quantum model is as accurate as the best classical models, not better. That matches the research paper: on this problem, quantum gives a comparable model with very few settings (50), not a speed or accuracy advantage.

Look inside the quantum circuits

What the classifier’s 4 qubits read out

Chance of each of the 16 possible readings |q₀q₁q₂q₃⟩. Only qubit 0 is used for the answer: purple bars have q₀ = 0 and red bars have q₀ = 1. ⟨Z₀⟩ = P(q₀=0) − P(q₀=1) is then turned into P(busy).

The classifier circuit (one of its 3 repeated layers)

RY(w·x) gates load the clues (encoding); the RZ·RY·RZ gates hold the learned settings θ; the CNOTs link the qubits in a ring (entanglement) so the clues can interact.

Plans the QAOA circuit outputs most often

How the detour problem becomes a QUBO

J(x) = P·Jbusy(x) + (1−P)·Jquiet(x) + pen·xe,   Jstate(x) = (V(1−s·x))² / C + (V s·x)² / Calt + τ V s·x

xi = 1 sends on-ramp i to the detour; si is the share of traffic entering at ramp i (assumed, since the dataset has one sensor). P is the quantum classifier’s chance of “busy”. Vbusy, Vquiet are the average volumes of busy and not-busy training hours, and Cbusy < Cquiet because a busy highway is near capacity. τ is the extra time of the detour, and the penalty keeps an ambulance’s ramp xe on the cleared highway. J is quadratic in the 0/1 variables x, so it is a QUBO; substituting xi = (1−Zi)/2 gives the cost Hamiltonian that QAOA minimises.

Real quantum chips make mistakes. We simulate a chip with all three main kinds of error at once, on both quantum parts (the classifier and the detour search), and then correct for them with cheap mitigation steps. The chip’s error rates are scaled by a noise level: 1 = typical of today’s superconducting chips, 2 = twice as noisy, and so on.

What is simulated, and where

ErrorWhere it happensLevel 1How we handle it
Gate errorsevery 1-qubit gate and every CNOT0.025% / 0.75%rescaling (below)
Decay (T1, T2)during every gate and while qubits wait150 µs / 100 µsrescaling
Readout errorswhen the qubits are measured1.5%readout correction
Shot noisefinite number of runs (1,024 / 256)—enough shots

Qubits are linked in a ring, as on real chips, so the detour circuit needs extra SWAP gates to connect distant qubits (— CNOTs after compiling). Readout correction: measure how often a 0 is read as 1 (and the reverse) once, then undo it. Rescaling: gate errors and decay shrink the classifier’s reading towards 0 by a steady factor; we measure that factor on 100 training hours and divide it out.

Why not quantum error correction?

Error correction (Experiments 7 and 8) spreads each qubit over several, e.g. 9 for Shor’s code. For our 4-qubit classifier that is 36+ qubits, and its rotation gates by arbitrary angles cannot be applied directly to encoded qubits; that needs fault-tolerant machinery far beyond today’s chips. With noisy gates and readout the correction circuit itself also adds errors. So, like most near-term quantum work, we use error mitigation instead: no extra qubits, a few calibration runs, and a classical correction.
